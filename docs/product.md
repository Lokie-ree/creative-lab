# Alignment Framework

Bridging discovery-first pedagogy with Louisiana Student Standards for Mathematics (LSSM).

---

## 1. Strategic convergence: pedagogy as the product

For EdTech leadership, the competitive value of a digital learning platform is rarely found in the technical "tool" itself, but rather in the **pedagogical architecture** that governs the student's interaction with it. Strategic alignment requires more than tagging content with state codes; it demands a transformation of the learning experience from passive consumption to active, standard-compliant cognitive labor. By mapping high-fidelity interactive design directly to state-mandated rigor, we resolve the problem of passive consumption that traditional systems fail to address—moving the product from a digital textbook to a **pedagogical engine** where understanding is the natural byproduct of exploration.

This framework rests on four core design pillars (canonical definitions in [philosophy.md](./philosophy.md)); this document maps them to state rigor and content clusters:

- **Discovery before formula** — Students engage with mathematical phenomena before being handed the formal shorthand.
- **Manipulation before explanation** — Learning begins with tactile or visual movement of mathematical objects, creating a mental model before verbal or written definitions.
- **The "earned reveal"** — Access to formal notation and symbolic representation is granted only after a student has established conceptual grounding.
- **Visual confirmation over multiple choice** — Feedback comes from the success of a visual construction or manipulation, not from selecting a static answer.

These principles target the three components of rigor defined by the Louisiana Department of Education: **Conceptual Understanding**, **Procedural Skill and Fluency**, and **Application**.

---

## 2. The rigor mandate: conceptual understanding and application

The LSSM "Guide to Rigor" requires that mathematics instruction balance conceptual depth with procedural fluency and the ability to apply math in context. Traditional media often struggles with "Conceptual Understanding" and "Application," reducing them to text-heavy explanations or rote word problems. Interactive experiences excel in these domains by letting students *see* the math in motion and interact with it as a dynamic system.

| Interactive principle   | LSSM alignment & justification |
|-------------------------|--------------------------------|
| **The earned reveal**   | **Conceptual understanding:** Ensures students grasp the "why" of a mathematical relationship before the "how" of its notation, preventing symbolic manipulation without meaning. |
| **Manipulation-first**  | **Procedural skill and fluency:** Builds an intuitive sense of operations (e.g., factoring, shifting graphs) before formalizing steps, so fluency is rooted in visual logic. |
| **Visual confirmation** | **Application:** Lets students validate their models against visual outcomes, mirroring real-world cycles of formulation and validation. |

The principle of *visual confirmation over multiple choice* supports the LSSM requirement for students to *"Construct a viable argument to justify a solution method"* (A1: A-REI.A.1). In an interactive environment, the student's manipulation is not just a "check"—it serves as the primary evidence for their justification. Standard A-REI.A.1 requires explaining each step from the assumption that a solution exists; visual confirmation provides the empirical grounding for that chain of reasoning and bridges to the specific demands of the Algebra I content cluster.

## 3. Algebra I: bridging abstract expressions and functional modeling

Algebra I is a critical gateway from simple identification of numbers to interpretation of complex mathematical structures. The strategic focus shifts from "identifying components" to **interpreting structure**, so students can view expressions as dynamic entities.

### Mapping standard clusters to discovery-first design

- **Seeing structure in expressions (A1: A-SSE.A.1/2)** — Discovery-first design lets students interpret complicated expressions by viewing parts as a "single entity" before symbolic notation. For example, interactive modules let students manipulate the term *(1+r)^n* in *P(1+r)^n* as a single factor. By visualizing this structure before notation is revealed, students satisfy A1: A-SSE.A.1b.
- **Interpreting functions (A1: F-IF.B.4/5)** — With manipulation-before-explanation, students explore how changing intercepts or end behavior relates to a function's quantitative relationship, reaching "Mastery" ALDs where they must relate domain to the quantitative relationship across linear, quadratic, and exponential functions.
- **Rate of change (A1: F-IF.B.6)** — To move from Level 3 (calculating from a table) to Level 5 "Advanced," design must go beyond computation. Interactive graphing supports "comparing rates of change associated with different intervals," so students can estimate and interpret changes from a graph in context.

As Algebra I masters the abstract structure of expressions, Geometry applies these insights through the visual and spatial demands of transformations.

## 4. Geometry: transformations as the engine of rigor

In the LSSM, Geometry moves from routine problem-solving toward **proving relationships among geometric figures**. The engine for this rigor is the use of transformations—rotations, reflections, and dilations—to define congruence and similarity.

Using the Sinewaves module and Trigonometry roadmap as a blueprint:

- **Similarity and trigonometry (GM: G-SRT.C.6)** — Similarity transformations with right triangles let students discover visually that side ratios are properties of the angles. This discovery-first path leads to definitions of sine and cosine for acute angles and fulfills the LSSM requirement to use similarity to define trigonometric ratios.
- **Congruence and transformations (GM: G-CO.A.2/5)** — The LSSM names "geometry software" as a tool to "describe transformations as functions that take points in the plane as inputs and give other points as outputs" (GM: G-CO.A.2). Digital modules let students specify a sequence of transformations that carry one figure onto another—functional, precise description rather than simple identification.

### Strategic advantage of discovery-first geometry (GM: G-SRT.A.2)

- **Active proof** — Students *decide* if figures are similar by enacting transformations rather than memorizing definitions.
- **Dynamic evidence** — Manipulation provides direct visual evidence of the "equality of all corresponding pairs of angles and the proportionality of all corresponding pairs of sides" required by the standard.
- **Conceptual anchoring** — Visual confirmation ensures proportionality is seen as a necessary consequence of dilation, not an arbitrary rule.

## 5. Achievement level acceleration: from basic to advanced

The Achievement Level Descriptors (ALDs) define student proficiency. Interactive experiences are well suited to move students from Level 3 (Basic) to Level 5 (Advanced).

| Level     | Performance characteristics                                      | Interactive design driver        |
|----------|-------------------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------|
| **Basic (Level 3)**   | Identifying components, routine calculating, identifying rational numbers. | Standard practice / traditional media |
| **Advanced (Level 5)** | Analyzing, justifying, comparing rates of change, modeling.       | Understanding precedes notation  |

The portfolio vision that **understanding precedes notation** is the primary driver for Level 5 performance. In Algebra I (Expressions), manipulation-before-explanation lets students discover the structure of a factorable quadratic through visual area models before they "write and analyze" it at Level 5.

The "Modeling & Application" standards require the basic modeling cycle: identifying variables, formulating a model, and validating conclusions. A student-first interface supports this cycle by reducing UI friction so students can focus on formulating and validating against visual feedback, without the intrusive calculation errors that often stall progress at Level 3.

---

## 6. Summary of strategic alignment

High-leverage learning experiences sit at the intersection of mathematical depth, classroom-tested pedagogy, and technical execution. By mapping interactive principles to LSSM mandates, we ensure the digital product is a **primary driver of student achievement**, not merely a supplement.

### Executive takeaways

1. **Pedagogical integrity** — Principles like "Discovery before formula" are strategic tools for meeting "Conceptual Understanding" in ways traditional media cannot.
2. **Scalable impact** — A well-designed module can move a student from Level 3 "Basic" (calculating) to Level 5 "Advanced" (analyzing and comparing) through manipulation and dynamic feedback.
3. **Standard compliance** — The LSSM explicitly calls for modern tools (e.g., geometry software, technology-based approximations) that this framework is built to provide and optimize.

> One teacher reaches 150 students; one well-designed learning experience reaches millions.
